I — The Choosing

No one watched the choosing.

There were acolytes enough for the masonry, for the hauling, for the copying of measures and the keeping of lamps, but this work I had done alone. The basin gave only what it was allowed to give. It did not open souls to me. It did not make saints legible or villains convenient. It showed surfaces, repetitions, the shape a life made in matter: hands, tools, rooms, pauses, the things a person reached for without thinking because they had reached for them a thousand times before. Names came attached to objects. A stitched pocket. A ration chit. A badge. A file header. A transit permit. A game login. I learned to read those scraps as if they were scripture.

I had spent years bringing the cathedral to the point where a request of this size could be held without killing the room. Even then, complete was too proud a word. It was complete enough. The columns stood. The choir vault held. The channels under the floor had been sealed and tested. The Vas Halidani held the metered Grace. The old prayers had been cut to fit the tolerances the engine required. All of that labor existed for one narrow fact: when the hour came, I would not take whoever the void handed me. I would choose.

The basin showed me Cole first in the way it often showed men like him: not at rest, but bent to a fault. Morenci, Arizona. Three in the morning. The open pit lay under sodium lamps, bench-terraces cut into the earth like a vast machine tooth by tooth. Flat orange light. Dust made dull by distance. A Komatsu 930E sat wrong on the ramp, big enough to make blockage into weather, its dead steering stick holding up everything behind it. Cole came up carrying a dented stainless coffee mug hooked in two fingers. He climbed as if ladders and handholds had entered his body years ago and stayed there. I watched him get into the belly of the thing, lean into the problem, back out, go in again. The fault was a hose chafed raw on a bracket that had been rubbing for weeks. I could see the shine where it had kissed metal, the black wetness beginning where it had finally given way. He did not rush because men were waiting below him and because rushing at weight is another name for carelessness. He patched it temporary, cribbed safe, walked it down.

Later the basin found him in the trailer. Boots off outside. Socks on thin flooring. Systems games until his eyes burned. He picked his own build. He did it with the same face he had worn over the broken machine: not joyless, not grim, simply attentive. Above the desk, written in heavy capitals on torn cardboard, was the rule he kept for himself: TOUCH NOTHING YOU HAVEN'T LOOKED AT TWICE. I read that more than once.

The basin took me to him again at the end of a break. He climbed into a 930E cab, mug still in hand, stainless steel dulled by dents and washing. His boot found the brake. He turned the key. That was the breath I stole him from.

The others settled by degrees.

Renata Salk I saw first under surgical light. Her name was stitched over her pocket in dark thread, the letters softened by laundering. She scrubbed to the elbows at a sink that had been used so long the metal around the taps was worn pale. Then she entered a room where a chest lay open under drapes and hard white light. She did not move like a believer in miracles. She moved like a woman who understood sequence, pressure, margin. A clamp changed hands. A sponge count was called. Her shoulders held steady for hours in a posture that would have punished any softer devotion.

Bohdan Cerny came to me in weather. A wall had gone out of truth and he was setting it back one stone at a time. Mortar shone wet on his trowel. His gloves were off; lime had lived long enough in the seams of his hands to pale them. He set, checked, tapped, scraped, reset. A plumb line hung against the work and did not flatter him. He made the line answer to the line.

Yuki Aritomo stood in rain with a tripod planted in bad ground. Her instrument was wrapped and unwrapped with the exactness of habit. She sighted, adjusted, wrote in a field book tucked under one arm, then sighted again. Surveyors look like they are doing little to the untrained eye. In truth they are arguing with error the whole time. I watched her choose where the world would admit being measured.

Wren Adebayo the basin showed me in a bread line long enough to become architecture. Coats, steam, a shuttered storefront, people bent into themselves against the cold. Wren stood midway down with a ration card in one hand and the other deep in a pocket gone shiny at the seam. When the line lurched, Wren did not crowd. When it stalled, Wren did not leave. Flour dust marked one cuff from some earlier handling of sacks or loaves; I could not tell which. The name was there on the card when the basin caught the angle: Wren Adebayo.

I saw others in those weeks. Stronger backs. More obvious violence. Quicker minds. Kinder faces. I passed over them and returned to these five. I do not tell myself the decision is clean because the need is real.

By the time I climbed the steps to the dais for the first rite, the names had settled in me with the heaviness of placed stone: Cole. Renata Salk. Bohdan Cerny. Yuki Aritomo. Wren Adebayo. I had not yet spoken them aloud. That was the last clean line I crossed.

II — The Rite

When the rite began, the cathedral already felt occupied by forces with no interest in incense or awe. The lamps were lit, yes. The choir stalls stood empty and severe. The basin was black as a closed eye. But under the floor there was pressure. In one ear I had the liturgy I had trained into my mouth until it could survive fear; in the other I had the sense of valves, thresholds, release. Priest and engineer met there, and neither excused the other.

The Vas Halidani held the Grace beneath the choir, counted, sealed, and metered. We had measured it three times before dawn. Nine thousand. Not allegory. Not promise. Fuel with a sacred name.

I opened the petition, and the room answered in the voice it always used when it answered at all: flat, immediate, indifferent to whether the hearer was kneeling.

I spoke the opening command because the rite required command, and command was the shape request took when spoken correctly. The first prayer had been a command; the first command was a request the world granted. My hands stayed on the stone lip of the dais because I wanted to feel whether the channels held.

I named the world. I named the year. I named the cohort wave.

Then I named them one by one. Not quickly. Not as items on a list. Each name had to be placed into the petition with its proper weight or the Discernment would fail and the failure would be honest.

The floor tightened under me. Somewhere below, the Grace moved from counted reserve to spent act.

I had expected, in youth, that if the System ever spoke to me in such a moment it would feel like revelation. It felt like accounting. That was more frightening, and more useful.

The basin did not open then; it inverted. The seeing I had used in choosing became a seam under tension. Across that seam, Morenci flashed once more in exactness so sharp it hurt: orange sodium light over bench-terraces; the 930E cab; Cole's empty hand still shaped to the mug it had held; boot on brake; key turning over. The pull took him there. On his side he heard the same plain verdict I saw mirrored through the petition.

The others came with the last visible motions of their own worlds still on them. Renata from under surgical light, one arm still lifted from a field of drapes and metal. Bohdan with mortar drying pale at the edge of his thumbnail. Yuki rain-dark at the shoulder where weather had found a seam in her outer layer. Wren with flour or dust at the cuff and the stance of someone who had been standing for a long time because standing was what the day required.

I had petitioned for a receiving chamber, for breathable air, for light at tolerable strength, for the brief mercies of orientation that keep summoning from becoming slaughter. I did not write those mercies into being. I asked, and the world committed what it accepted.

Then they were there. Five human bodies in the first light of the first chamber, each carrying the ruinously ordinary evidence of where I had taken them from. No thunder. No choir. The rite did not beautify itself for my sake. It closed around facts: mass transferred, bond established, cost paid.

I finished the liturgy because unfinished mechanisms are more dangerous than cruel ones. My voice sounded less like a priest's than I had expected. It sounded like a man reading load figures while the bridge took weight.

The pressure under the floor began, slowly, to fall.

III — The silence after the rite closes

There was a silence after the last line cleared that I have never heard duplicated, though many rites followed. It was not peace. It was not triumph. It was the sound of a system settling after commit, of channels cooling, of a room discovering that what had only been design and rehearsal a moment before had become history.

I stood where I was and listened to the stone.

Below, the Vas Halidani was lighter by nine thousand Grace. The meter had done what it was built to do. The city was larger by five souls. That arithmetic was true before any of us had earned the right to interpret it.

Beyond the chamber, others would already be moving: attendants with blankets, water, script boards, the first crude attempts at welcome and containment. I did not go to them at once. There is a kind of cowardice in refusing to look at what one has made; there is another in rushing to manage the human consequences before one has admitted, if only to oneself, what the making cost. I stayed long enough to feel the floor cool through my sandals and to hear the last ticking in the channels die away.

I incur continuation. A system built to save can become a machine that requires saving to continue.

I knew that then. Not in theory. In weight. Five names had gone from observed lives to held responsibility, and the city would answer by asking what those five could build, defend, heal, measure, bear. Need would dress itself in gratitude if allowed. Necessity would begin calling itself virtue if not watched closely. The order around me was already prepared to say that the first successful rite proved its own righteousness. Orders are always prepared to say that.

I did not say it.

I also did not repent in any clean or useful way. The siege had not lifted because I doubted. The dead ground had not become arable because I hesitated. The walls had not thickened on pity alone. I had asked the world for people and the world had granted them. That remained true whether the asking was salvation or conscription or some unstable alloy of the two.

When at last I left the dais, the names were still in me, but differently. Not candidates. Not observations in a basin. Entries in a ledger, yes, but also absences with shape: a mug not carried, a clamp handed to no one, a stone left waiting for the next strike, a measurement interrupted, a line for bread closing over a place where a person had stood.

The silence did not absolve me. It did not accuse me either. It simply remained, while beyond it the first cohort of Bastion learned that I had called them and that the world had answered.
