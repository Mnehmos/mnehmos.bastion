Residual heat slicks the joint, but the real signal is colder: the grain inside the slab wants to creep, half a millimeter at a time, toward the brass nail-line that borders the dais. A floor can ask for relief the way a lung asks for air; right now the chamber is holding its breath.

III — Vas Halidani

Vas Halidani.

The name fits the void under everything, the way a vaulted cistern's curve carries both water and load. My palm had already mapped it: hollow cylinder, forty cubits deep, ringed with radiating galleries. Hearing the word drops mortar into every joint of the mental drawing. The plan is complete now — labeled, oriented, trustworthy.

The limestone under me feels different once named, as if legal title just changed hands from rumor to record. The structure no longer asks for relief; it knows its place.

My right knee straightens first. The left follows when the slab offers no objection. I press off the heel of my hand, print of brass dust left in my skin's whorls.

All of it tells one story: someone built for centuries, someone else just shook the foundations, and my cohort stands in the after-ring like chisel-marks still warm.

I draw a slow breath, square to Mnehmos. The floor holds by more than reputation now; it holds by name.

I rest one hand on the hammer looped at my belt — not to draw it, only to remember who I am when stone speaks. The city is real, the risks are real, and the cistern below us has a name that already feels like history under pressure.

Whatever work waits beyond these walls, I will meet it on the level, eye, and plumb.
