Earth. A world spending itself to death. Before the wrong room — there was just the work.

Mid-shift, the pit's air tastes like copper dust and moved earth. The right rear track has buckled, dragging a dull squeal up through the cab — or maybe it's just the echo of a long night. I slide under the machine, oil warm on my forearms, grease dark on my knuckles. The pin is worn to a blunt edge, the spacer a sliver too loose, the teeth of the sprocket catching on mud. I pry, I hammer, I twist the last clamp tight, I chase the wobble out of the ride. The track settles, the slack goes quiet, I measure against the spec in my head, then again with a flashlight and a wrench. It's not a miracle, and it's not a show — just the sense that something is finally right, that the beast won't grind to a halt when the clock screams.

The shift wears off like a skin you can peel away when you're not looking. I'm in a quiet trailer, a lamp haloing a bedroll and a battered desk. Patch notes flicker on a tablet, and I read them like a map: nerfs and buffs, how to push the numbers to the edge without breaking the line. I grade routes in my head, min-max the angle of every step, run little simulations of where to put my next move so the shift never loses momentum on a Sunday night. The room is empty except for the drone of a monitor and the echo of a distant work bell. It's peaceful in a place that shouldn't be peaceful. I tell myself I'm fine with that — the solitude, the quiet, the certainty that the next move has to be mine.

I carry a dented coffee mug with a chipped lip; I've had it since the first year of the job. It's the shape of a kind of luck I don't pretend to believe in, but I'll drink from it anyway. I keep it in a pocket of the old canvas bag, tucked where the heat of it will wake me up and the weight of a day's worth of small decisions won't slip away.

Then the world took him — boot still on the brake, the ramp still ahead. No transition.

Stone room, and it isn't a room at all. The air sits heavy, a quiet charge that makes the hair on my arms stand up. My heart drums in my chest, a struck wrench against thin metal. I don't know where I am, or why the world feels mapped to someone else's rules. I don't know the faces around me, or what any of this means. I only know I am not leaving this place with anything intact if I can help it.

Within arm's reach on the stone lies a fallen censer — a heavy brass thing meant to swing on a chain, vents seized shut, the chain kinked into a bird's-nest, the cup inside dead and cold. It's the only thing I recognize here, a broken mechanism staring back at me like a stubborn bolt I can still coax loose.

My hands move before my head catches up. A foreman's hands do their own thinking, and I'm remembering that as they slide out toward the censer. I wrap fingers around the cold brass, find the hinge, feel the weight of it settle into my palm. I test the seam where the vents should move, then press, trying to coax the stubborn metal to yield. The chain creaks, a dry protest, and I lean in, thumb brushing the edge of the vent as I search for any give. I grip tighter, prise, and bend the old iron a fraction toward freedom. I listen to the metal complain and answer it in kind. My hands go to the censer, and I begin to work it.

Numbers glare behind my eyes, a list I don't know how to read except as patch notes written in a language I've learned to trust: COLE MADDOX — summoned — Level 1. HP 12/12, AC 12, STR 13, DEX 11, CON 14, INT 13, WIS 16, CHA 10. Clean, precise, and totally out of place in a room that smells of stone and cold air. I blink, and the digits don't flicker; they sit there, a tiny, indifferent squadron of truths.

The censer rests warm in my hand, vents breathing again, chain running smooth, the cup seating right as if it never forgot how. It feels almost normal — the way a part you've coaxed back from a graveyard of broken things feels normal. Almost. My other hand finds a small, stubborn bit of metal near the wall — a loose latch on a debris-wrapped bracket — and I wedge it, pry at it, tune the thing until the metal cooperates, a slow, careful re-seat that isn't flashy but is true. The fix settles under my hands, and the room seems to exhale a fraction, the air warming a hair where the censer sits.

The readout stays quiet, then returns to its own patient stillness — as if it's deciding whether to show me more, or to wait until I've earned the right to be told. I'm a man who reads patch notes. You don't trust a system you haven't tested. So I test it. Slow, the way you reach for a wire you think is live.
