Read the Plate

My boot kept hunting for a brake pedal that wasn't there.

That was the first stupid, useful thing my body did. Not panic. Not prayer. Just right heel twitching on cold stone, trying to find the place it knew, the narrow travel of rubber under a work boot, the bite-point where sixty tons listened if you asked right and killed you if you asked wrong.

There was no cab. No console glow. No haul road crawling down in the dark with berms on one side and black drop on the other. No radio static. No burnt-coffee stink from the dented mug I kept wedged where it didn't quite fit in the 930E.

The mug wasn't with me.

That landed harder than it had any goddamn right to. Not because it was worth anything. It had a crease down one side from a tailgate, old copper dust in the seam, my name sharpied twice and half-gone. But it was mine. It belonged to a world where objects stayed where you set them down unless gravity, thieves, or stupid people got involved.

Here, nothing belonged.

Stone under my palms. Cold enough to wake the bones. Air full of ozone and church incense and the inside of a bell after somebody hit it hard. The pressure in the room was wrong, dropping through the soles of my boots like a compressor bleeding down through a bad valve. Above that, voices had just stopped. Or something like voices. Twenty-four people stood in stalls around the chamber, all of them looking like they'd held a live cable for an hour and didn't dare let go.

And inside my skull, still stamped there like a warning tag on a lockout hasp:

⟦ CLASS: PALADIN · BOUND ⟧

I stayed down for one breath longer than pride liked.

Pride gets men killed. Breath doesn't.

My hands checked me before my head caught up. Knees. Ribs. Belt. Pockets. No phone. No knife. No keys. Hard hat gone. Radio gone. No gloves. No mug. I was in my work clothes, boots and denim and shirt damp at the collar from an Arizona night that was supposed to have kept going without me.

Four others had come in with me.

I didn't know them. Didn't know if they were hurt, hostile, or just as screwed as I was.

One woman had an arm raised, palm-up, eyes moving body to body in a way I recognized even before I had a job title for it. Triage eyes. What's bleeding, what's breathing, what's about to stop.

A big man was down on one knee with his palm flat to the stone, not praying. Reading it. Joint, seam, bearing, crack. Mason, maybe. Somebody who trusted surfaces more than speeches.

Another stood with her head tilted a few degrees off plumb, rain-dark across one shoulder, an oilcloth book tucked under her arm like she'd been pulled out of weather mid-step. Her eyes were taking bearings off everything that didn't move.

The last was already up. Already placing herself where she could see the doors, the dais, the rest of us. Not aggressive. Positioned. There's a difference. She had the look of somebody who had spent too much life making a little stretch far enough and knew where bodies needed to stand before the bodies knew it.

Up on the dais were two figures who were not in stalls. One man at center. One woman to his right. The room bent around them, socially if not physically. That was the kind of thing you didn't need magic to read. Boss and knife.

I did not get up fast.

I got up careful.

First rule: touch nothing you haven't looked at twice.

Second rule: if the ground is talking, shut up and listen.

Something in me reached for the old habit — not fear exactly, not thought exactly. The thing that used to run while I stood over a dead dozer at three in the morning with a pissed-off superintendent on the radio and a storm building over the pit. What can kill us first. What controls actually reduce that. What's bullshit paperwork and what's the only reason we go home.

Only this time the habit looked back.

I stopped moving.

Not froze. Stopped.

There was a brass nail-line in the floor. I had seen it and not seen it, same way a new hand sees a berm and thinks decoration. It ran through the slabs under us, pretty as survey work, deadly as a highwall crack if the read was right.

And the read had come with labels.

Elimination. Substitution. Engineering. Administrative. PPE.

Mine language. Safety language. The hierarchy they beat into you until it sounded like corporate ass-covering, except the order was right because physics didn't care about the logo on the PowerPoint. Best hazard is gone. Second best is replaced. Third is guarded. Fourth is procedure. Last is hoping your hard hat saves you from something that should never have reached your head.

Something had just taken the inside of my skull and printed a field report in it.

I swallowed once.

"Okay," I said, because sometimes you say the little word to keep from saying the big ones. My voice sounded rough and too ordinary in that impossible room. "Everybody off the brass line."

None of them knew me. Hell, I didn't know me at the moment. But I pointed down, not at faces. Faces make it a contest. Floor makes it a fact.

"Line in the stone. Step clear of it. Now, not dramatic."

I moved first. Slow enough not to spook anyone, far enough to put two clean slabs between my boots and that nail-line. I kept my hands visible. Empty hands are underrated tools.

The woman with triage eyes clocked the movement and the line. The big man on one knee had already found it or found something cousin to it; his palm shifted off the joint. The rain-wet surveyor's eyes followed my point, then tracked the line's run across the chamber. The positioned woman moved like she'd been waiting for the first sane instruction and was annoyed it had taken three seconds to arrive.

Good.

Not safe. Safer.

There's a difference men die ignoring.

The twenty-four in the stalls stayed where they were. Two with hand tremor. I filed it. If one dropped, that was a falling-body problem, maybe a ritual problem, maybe a don't-touch-the-live-transformer problem. I looked toward the woman on the dais's right because every room has someone who owns the emergency even if they didn't write the procedure.

"Your people," I said, and nodded at the stalls. "Two are shaking."

Maybe she understood the words. Maybe the room translated. Maybe I was talking to a wall with cheekbones. But I'd made the callout. Administrative control. Signal the responsible party.

Then I had nothing to do for half a second, and the thing in my skull waited like a cursor.

⟦ CLASS: PALADIN · BOUND ⟧

II — Read the Plate

Paladin.

Of all the goddamn words.

I knew the shape of it from games, not from any church I'd ever bothered disappointing. Armor. Smite. Aura. Lay hands. Charisma tax. Oath features. Frontline half-caster with nova turns and party utility. Strong if built right. Annoying if built by someone who thought "righteous" was a personality.

But I had not built it.

That was the splinter.

I had rolled a thousand characters. Point buy, standard array, weird class mods, busted indie systems with six pages of errata and a dev who thought "intentional friction" meant bad math. I read patch notes for fun. I hunted breakpoints because every ruleset has a seam and seams are where leverage lives.

I chose my builds.

Always.

Strength or dex? Dump stat? Subclass path? Early feat or ability score? What's the party need and what's the campaign actually going to punish? I made those calls. I owned the consequences.

Now something had rolled for me.

Not offered. Not drafted. Rolled.

Bound.

I felt a laugh climb up and killed it before it got out. Wasn't funny enough. Was too funny. Both get people looking at you wrong.

I looked up toward the ceiling I didn't understand and kept my voice low.

"What am I now?"

The answer arrived flat, clean, and without one ounce of concern for my opinion.

I stared at numbers no eye was seeing.

Cole Maddox. It had my name. Morenci wasn't in that block, but it sat behind every digit like red dust in boot stitching.

Level one. Paladin. Oath Unsworn.

HP twelve. AC twelve.

That was not a lot of anything.

Strength thirteen, dex eleven, con fourteen, int thirteen, wisdom sixteen, charisma ten.

I had notes.

First note: whatever made this sheet either knew me better than I liked or had a sense of humor drier than mine. Wisdom sixteen. Fine. I'd spent twenty years learning how ground fails, how machines lie right before they break, how men get quiet before doing something stupid. Call that wisdom if you need a column filled.

Charisma ten. Fair. I could tell a crew what needed doing. I could not sell a drowning man a life jacket if he objected to the color.

Strength thirteen was workable, not heroic. Dex eleven was a shrug. Con fourteen I'd earned the old way: bad coffee, worse hours, and refusing to quit before the job was locked out, tagged out, and actually fixed. Int thirteen, sure. I could read schematics and patch notes. Half the world thinks that's genius because half the world won't read.

Saves: Wisdom, Charisma.

Divine Sense one of one. Lay on Hands five of five. Slots zero of zero. Oath locks at level three.

That last line sat there with teeth in it.

Unsworn, but bound. No oath, but a class whose whole damn chassis usually ran on one. No spells, no prepared list, no smite button surfaced, no armor, no weapon. Just a label, a couple of limited-use tools, and the promise of future commitment if I survived long enough for the rules to tighten.

The gamer in me was appalled.

The operator in me said: spec is spec. Test under load.

You do not curse a machine into being the model you wanted. You walk around it. You read the plate. You check leaks, guards, pins, hoses, alarms. Then you find out what it'll do without putting a man under the bucket.

I did not fire the Divine Sense.

The read had suggested it. One use. Unknown baseline. Consecration field pushing back at the edge of attention like glare on glass. Tempting as hell. But one-of-one is not a toy, and I was standing in a room full of unknowns with the people who had dragged me here still upright.

Save the ping until it changes a decision.

Lay on Hands, though.

Five total. Tiny pool. Could be literal healing. Could be disease or poison removal if the wording held. Could be nothing like the tabletop version because "paladin" here might mean "congratulations, you're a themed battery." But the line was there.

The rain-wet surveyor was still wet.

Cold room. Unknown exposure. No shivering yet, which was not the comfort people think it is. She wasn't panicking. She was taking bearings, maybe because that was her job, maybe because shock had grabbed the steering wheel and chosen professionalism as camouflage.

I knew that trick. Used it myself.

III — One Point

I stepped toward her, but not into her space. Hands where she could see them.

"You're soaked," I said. "Cold room. Bad combination."

Her eyes came to me, sharp and measuring. Up close, the rain on her shoulder had darkened the cloth almost black. She had the field-book tucked under one arm like losing it would cost more than comfort. That, too, I understood.

I pointed toward the lamp-ring — warmer light, less draft, off the brass nail-line. "Move there. Keep the wet layer open if you can. Don't strip unless you've got dry under it. Wet cloth in cold air'll steal heat faster than you think."

I paused.

"I've got… something. Maybe a first-aid trick, maybe bullshit. I don't know yet. I'm not touching you unless you say yes."

That was important. Maybe the most important thing I'd said since arriving. People in shock need choices that are real and small. Yes or no. Step here. Hold this. Breathe. Don't let the world become one big hand grabbing them.

She looked from me to the lamp-ring, then to the floor line, then back. Her chin dipped once. Permission, or close enough to permission that I still moved slow.

I put two fingers against the back of my own hand first, because if there was going to be a light show or a burn or some righteous nonsense, I wanted warning. Nothing.

Then I touched her sleeve at the rain-dark shoulder. Not skin. Cloth. A mechanic's compromise with a miracle.

"One point," I muttered. "Diagnostic only."

Warmth moved through my hand.

Not heat, exactly. Not like a torch or blood returning to a numb finger. It had intent in it, which is a hell of a thing to say about a sensation. It went out of me cleanly and left a measurable absence behind, like watching a fuel gauge tick down one notch.

Her shoulders eased by maybe half an inch.

Maybe that was the effect. Maybe that was because somebody had finally done a small practical thing instead of chanting at her from a stall.

The System — and I was thinking of it with a capital letter now because a thing that prints debits in your head earns a noun — did not lie. It also did not flatter. Mitigated at the margin. Not eliminated. Wet clothing remains active hazard.

Good.

No miracle where procedure belonged.

"Still need the wet dealt with," I said. "That didn't dry you. Just bought a little margin."

I took my hand back and flexed my fingers once. Nothing numb. No visible glow lingering. No choir. Thank Christ, or whoever was taking tickets in this place.

The woman with triage eyes had watched the whole thing. Surgeon, medic, butcher, something adjacent — her attention had sharpened when I touched the wet sleeve. I gave her a small nod, the universal sign for I know you saw that and no, I don't have the manual either.

The big mason had not stopped reading the floor. Good man. If the world is ending, somebody should keep an eye on the slab.

The positioned woman had shifted again so the five of us weren't clumped like idiots on a target. I liked her immediately and trusted that not at all.

Above us, the man on the dais still had not descended.

Mnehmos, the read had named him, though I had not heard anyone speak it. It bothered me that the name had come attached to the hazard report. It bothered me more that the report had returned him as above resolution. In mining terms, that meant my instrument wasn't rated for the load. In gaming terms, skull icon over the health bar. In human terms, don't start shit with the forklift until you know whether it has brakes.

The woman at his right — Severa, same source, same unease — had been signaled about the tremors. If she ignored it, that told me something. If she moved, that told me something else.

My head wanted to split into boards and strings.

One part of me was still in Arizona, mad about a coffee mug and wondering whether the truck had rolled without me, whether my empty cab had ghosted down-ramp until physics noticed the missing foot. Another part was counting exits, lines, loads, people, tremors, heat loss. Another was staring at the sheet and already building around it despite the insult of not choosing it.

Paladin, level one.

No oath. No spells. Limited heal. One sense ping. Decent wisdom. Mediocre charisma. If this really mapped anywhere near the rules I knew, early survivability depended on equipment, party shape, and not pretending level one made you special. Level one is where rats crit you and the obituary calls it tragic.

But the class was mine now in the same way a rented machine becomes yours when it's on your cut and the storm is coming. You don't have to love it. You have to learn its noises.

I looked down at my hands.

They were still my hands. Scar across the left knuckle from a hose clamp. Thumbnail with a black crescent grown almost out from a bad pinch. Grease that never fully left the cuticles. Not holy hands. Not knight hands.

Mine.

"Cole Maddox," I said, mostly to the four others, partly to the room, partly to keep the name from becoming just another line on a sheet. "Morenci, Arizona. Heavy equipment."

A beat.

"And I don't know what the hell this is either."

That was as much speech as I had in me.

The pressure under the floor kept falling. The smell of ozone thinned under the incense. The stalls held their exhausted people. The dais held its impossible man. My lost mug stayed lost in a cab on another world, if that world was still allowed to be called that.

Somewhere behind my eyes, the ledger sat quiet.

Participation trophies were bullshit anyway.

The first hour closed without me understanding the machine. The second began with one fewer point in the tank, four strangers still breathing, and the System in the chair.

The first hour was a diagnostic. The second hour is where the machine starts being tested under load. He has 2/3 attentional_capacity, 4/5 Lay on Hands, 1/1 Divine Sense — and four people he hasn't named yet standing closer to him than anyone has stood in years.
