The rite had closed like a lid.

I was still in the posture from the surgical room — gloved-and-ready, arm raised, palm-up — standing on the receiving floor of a room that smelled wrong for any hospital I knew. Too dry in the air. Too much iron in the stone. Vapor feathered low over the slabs as if the chamber were breathing out. The slabs themselves held heat — brass nail-lines marking the load axis, hot enough that the skin of my forearm prickled through the glove seam.

I did not lower my hand yet.

First instinct after any shock is a count. Not names. Not stories. Bodies, then risk.
:::

:::cap
I — Triage
:::

:::panel
I scanned the four who'd arrived with me — surface only, no interiors, no guesses beyond what can kill you fast.

One: **a wet-clothed surveyor** stood conserving motion. Their garments clung where water had soaked them. No open wound visible from here. No pooling. No active bleeding that would darken the brass lines. The hazard wasn't blood — yet. It was exposure load: cold retained in fabric, muscle stiffness, delayed collapse.

Two: **a heavy-equipment man** balanced like the floor was lying to him. He kept shifting his weight in small corrections, eyes on the slab seams. Alert. No immediate hemorrhage. No obvious breathing compromise from my angle.

Three: **a mason** — one knee at the slab, palm close as if listening to stone. Dust on their hands. Their posture read *trained stillness*, not fainting. No acute limb hazard visible.

Four: **a woman with chosen sightlines** — she'd positioned for visual coverage, flour-dust on a cuff. She looked ready to inform someone, not to be treated.

I keep myself last. It's discipline, not modesty.

Up on the dais: the man who performed the rite stood where the working had come from, and a woman beside him held a closed instrument like she'd been issued precision and nothing else. I wouldn't touch either of them. I wouldn't even try to read them. Ceremony is a vector for missed differentials. Sentimentality kills more reliably than blood.
:::

:::panel
The chamber itself gave off information without language.

Twenty-four robed summoners stood in stalls around the perimeter. Their pose was professional — no wide-eyed awe, no flinch at the heat. They were waiting for procedures, not miracles.

Under the floor: subtle pressure-release, venting the last tension from the rite-strike. The air vibrated with a slow release, like a wound dressing settling.

My understanding-of-language was provisional. I heard meaning without relying on phonetics. That matters. It means my next sentence must be short enough to survive ambiguity.

I finally brought my attention to the only question that counts in the first minute: **what's bleeding, what's breathing, what's about to fail**.

No one on my floor grid showed active hemorrhage. The brass lines were hot. The chamber was cooling.

And in the back of my mind — some clerk part of me that refused to be useful — another fact surfaced: *the order isn't assigned yet.* In a room like this, the order is the only thing that can be murdered.
:::

:::panel
I did not get an answer yet. Not in words I could trust. So I used the only tool that doesn't care what tongue is spoken: **triage tags based on observable hazard.**

I held the arm-up count long enough to watch for immediate collapse. Still no one went down. Still no sudden bleeding. Still that slow vent under the floor.

Then I let my gaze flick to the brass lines.

Hot. Load axis. Nursing an injury here would be a mistake you can't sew up — wrong step, wrong fall, wrong time.

:::panel
No magic. No gifts. Just the body I brought and the skills I didn't forget.

That meant triage stayed triage. I could do what surgeons do: observe, classify, stabilize, and delegate what can be delegated. If the chamber demanded rites and titles, that was beyond my first minute.

I checked breathing by watching chest rise and the way shoulders held tension. Nothing screamed *airway compromise*. Nothing showed *catastrophic bleed*. The wet-clothed surveyor remained the nearest risk — not from visible wounds, but from the way cold fabric steals heat and coordination.

If someone collapses because muscle seizes, you don't wait for sentiment. You move them toward warmth. Lamp ring, heat source, anything safe from the brass line.
:::

:::cap
III — Voice
:::

:::panel
The chamber waited.

On the dais, the officiant stood as if the rite had completed him rather than exhausted him. The instrument-holding woman beside him stayed closed and composed. Professional. Not ceremonial flourish.

On the floor, the four with me held their positions like they'd practiced the geometry. Their faces were turned toward the center, toward the dais, toward any cue that might become instruction.

And then, near the end of my first window — near enough that it changed the air before it changed my plan — I heard another voice in the chamber, speaking in my direction with urgency:
:::

:::bubble who="cohort-male"
Everybody off the brass line.
:::

:::panel
I recognized only the meaning. Not the speaker.

The directive landed like a scalpel: simple, absolute, and grounded in physics.

The brass lines were hot. They were also load-axis markings, not where you stand while being transferred between states. Hot plus mechanical stress equals burns and falls.

My hand finally began to come down — but not in surrender. In transition. Count complete, hazard corrected.

I kept my gaze on the wet-clothed surveyor. Exposure load is an enemy that doesn't announce itself with blood.

I spoke once, professional and short, using the only kind of warmth I trusted: actionable warmth.
:::

:::bubble who="Renata"
You. Wet cloth — move toward the lamp ring. Keep your feet off the brass.
:::

:::panel
I did not touch them. Not until I asked and got consent. In a room full of procedure, a grab can become a complication you didn't earn.

I watched their response for hesitation — cold-brain delays are common when nerves go numb. If they froze, I would switch from instruction to safer repositioning: step by step, with permission. If they moved, I would keep them within my visual lane and monitor for the first signs of shivering muscle collapse — teeth clench, tremor, failing grip.

I glanced once at the heavy-equipment man and the mason, tracking whether they'd already reacted to the "brass line" warning. The heavy-equipment man was reading the slab seams — likely to step away quickly. The mason's knee at the slab meant the decision had to be explicit. Knees bruise, burns follow, and falls start with one wrong angle.

The woman with sightlines didn't seem physically endangered from my angle, but her stance mattered. If she drifted into the wrong zone looking, she'd become a second hazard.

The dais did not move yet. The summoners remained in their stalls. No bodies surged. No stampede. That told me something: the chamber's control was still working.

Still.

I lowered my arm to my side fully, palm now facing inward under the glove, and completed the cycle: triage scan done, sheet verified, voice applied.

No one had bled out in the first minute. That's not luck. That's procedure.

I stood where I arrived, in the receiving geometry, and waited for the next cue — an injury that required a hand, or an instruction that required a sentence shorter than fear.
:::
