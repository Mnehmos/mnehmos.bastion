:::cap
I — The Choosing
:::

:::panel tone
No one watches the choosing.

They will watch the calling. They will come when the lamps are raised and the nave is full of breath and expectation, when the twenty-four stand in their stalls and the Grace under the floor answers the weight of spoken names. They will say that is the holy hour. They will say that is where the burden lies.

It does not begin there.
:::

:::panel
The cathedral is finished enough. A builder learns to respect that phrase. Finished enough means the load will bear. It means the vault may still show scaffold marks and one transept still smell of lime, but the lines hold, the channels are true, the cisterns under the flagstones are sealed, and what is gathered below will not leak away when asked to rise. Grace sits beneath me now in its hidden veins, patient and expensive. Stone has memory. So does cost.

The stalls are empty. The doors are barred. The city sleeps in the measure that cities under siege sleep. I stand alone at the basin of black glass and lay ash on the lip with two fingers. Salt. Water. The thin filings of silver. What I do here is not command. I have learned that too well to pretend otherwise. I ask to be shown, and the law beneath the world decides how much sight I am granted.
:::

:::panel
The surface darkens, then takes depth.

Worlds do not open like books. They do not offer themselves. They arrive in fragments, in angles, in recurring gestures. A shoulder bent under a load. A hand on a valve. Mud on a stair. Blood washed from knuckles into a basin. A face seen only when it turns toward a sound outside the frame. I never have their thoughts. I am spared that, or denied it. What I am given is enough to damn me or absolve me in pieces, and not enough to settle which.
:::

:::panel
I return, as I have returned for weeks, to the mine on the copper world.

Its light is the wrong color, thin and spent, as though the sun itself has been worked too hard. The machine bays sweat. Every surface wears dust. Somewhere beyond what I am shown, the world is using up the last of what it can burn to keep itself moving. I see the man again: coveralls half-zipped, lamp strapped crooked, shoulders narrower than the work asks for. His crew call him from a catwalk where two others have stopped dead around a silent conveyor. He does not hurry for drama. He comes at the pace of a man who means to arrive useful.

I watch him kneel in black slurry. Watch him put his ear to housing, then his hand. Watch him send one worker away with two fingers, call another closer, take a wrench from her without looking at her face. He slides under the machine until only his boots show. There is a pause. Then his arm reaches back into view, palm open for a different tool. When he comes out again there is grease across his throat and one cheek. The iron wakes. The belt shudders, catches, runs. Nobody cheers. One man thumps the housing once and goes back to work. The one beneath the machine wipes his hand on a rag already too dirty to matter, lifts a dented mug from where he left it on a crate, drinks whatever has gone cold inside, and moves on.

Later — if later is the true word for these stolen views — I see him alone in a trailer lit by one bad lamp. He sits at a narrow table with a manual open beside a stripped component and a pencil worn down so far it has become almost all metal ferrule. He reads, stops, turns back three pages, reads again. He draws a line on scrap. Crosses it out. Draws another. No one sees him there. No one interrupts him. If he vanished between one shift and the next, there are worlds in which the ledger would notice before the room did.

I let that sight go. Not because it is light, but because if I keep looking only at him I will begin to lie to myself about what I am doing.
:::

:::panel
The basin gives me the surgeon. A tent first, canvas bellied with wind. Then a room of tile and failing light. Then a corridor where men in gray uniforms sit against a wall and hold cloth to themselves with both hands. In every place, the same woman. Sleeves rolled. Apron dark to the waist. Hair cut short because there is no time in her world to be precious with what can carry filth. I never see hesitation in her hands. I see a basin of water turned pink, then red, then changed. I see her step aside long enough to scrub her forearms to the elbow and step back in before the water stills.

The mason comes next. Rain on stone. Then sun on stone. Then sleet on stone. Years passing only because the wall changes and breaks and rises and breaks again. He taps each block before it joins the course. He rejects more than the foreman likes. Once I see him standing before a section shattered outward, rubble in the street, a child's wheelbarrow half-buried under mortar. He says nothing I can hear. He only bends, lifts, sorts salvage from ruin, and sets the line again with a cord pulled taut between two nails.

The surveyor is all instruments and patience. Tripod legs pressed into frost. Brass catching a false dawn. There are plains on her world where distance lies to the eye. She trusts the rod, the glass, the figures she writes in a notebook wrapped in oilcloth. I see her stop a wagon train with one raised hand, sight along the level again, then move them ten yards left to ground that looks no safer and is.

Last, tonight, the woman at the bread-line. Snow in the gutters gone gray with feet. She stands on an overturned crate behind two battered vats, ladle in one hand, the other hand free. The free hand interests me. It counts loaves. It points children behind older bodies. It takes a wrist and turns a knife aside when the line buckles and men surge forward all at once. Not dramatically. Efficiently. She does not chase him. She puts the next loaf into the next pair of hands and restores the line by inches, by posture, by the refusal to give the crowd her fear in a form they can use.
:::

:::panel
There are others. There are always others.

That is part of the obscenity of it. Selection is not merely taking some; it is leaving the rest where they stand. I keep lists. I strike through names. I return to certain lives and not to others. The city will one day tell these stories as if necessity itself descended and chose the worthy. It will be kinder that way. But kindness is not accuracy. I know the shape of my part in this. I ask to see. I look. I decide.

I do not tell myself the decision is clean because the need is real.

The basin clouds. My allotted sight thins. I could ask again, spend a little more, take one more circuit through the worlds I have been weighing. Instead I let the dark glass go black and still. I have looked long enough to know that further looking is only a different form of delay.
:::

:::panel
On the table beside me lies the cedar board scored with shallow grooves for ink. I write the names in order, not because the order will save me from what they mean, but because order is the only honest shape I know to give a thing this grave.

**Cole Maddox.**
**Renata Salk.**
**Bohdan Cerny.**
**Yuki Aritomo.**
**Wren Adebayo.**

The letters dry. Beneath the floor, Grace waits in its channels. Around me the unfinished cathedral holds its breath with stone patience. I place my palm over the board and feel the grooves under my skin as if they were cuts. The names are settled in me now. I stand at the threshold of the rite and do not yet speak.
:::

:::cap
II — The Rite
:::

:::panel
I place my hand on the black lip of the speaking basin. The stone is colder than the air. The runes cut around it have been inked and re-inked until they seem less written than fed. The Vocatio Sebastina is old enough now to sound ancient, though this form of it is younger than the siege. Everything durable in Bastion is revised under pressure.
:::

:::bubble who="Archvigil Severa di Mauritz"
Ready.
:::

:::panel
…and then she enters the Discernment. Nine seconds is a very short span in which to ask whether one should alter a world. I do not pray for permission. That would be cleaner than the truth. I pray for legibility.

When the nine seconds pass, she opens her eyes. She does not absolve me. She never has. She gives the smallest motion of assent: the line is there; the channels answer; the petition may be made. So I begin.

I speak the first clause of the Vocatio, and as always it strikes me that liturgy and mechanism are only two distances from the same act. Prayer, in other words, as engineering. My voice goes out into the nave and comes back changed by stone. Behind me, the twenty-four take their parts in sequence. I give the names. Not loudly. Precisely. Each one is a cut in me. To call a person by true intention is to accept that if they come, the wound of that fact belongs to you.
:::

:::bubble who="Mnehmos"
Take notice. Receive this Grace. Install these named persons into Bastion under the protections and severances our law permits — with memory continuous, with will intact, with translation sufficient for immediate survival, with no false peace laid over the violence of transit. Let them arrive as themselves. Let the city be enlarged by what it must answer for.
:::

:::panel
The basin trembles once beneath my hand as the metering deepens. Nine thousand Grace is not a metaphor when it moves; it is pressure, deprivation elsewhere, stored possibility becoming spent fact. If the petition is denied, the asking still costs us. If it is granted, the cost only becomes visible in a different ledger.

I know what else I incur. Not only the Grace. I incur continuation. If this works, there will be a next cohort, and a next architecture to support them, and more of the world made answerable to the need that justified this one. A system built to save can become a machine that requires saving to continue. I know this before I open my mouth. I open it anyway.
:::

:::bubble who="Mnehmos"
I petition the System to commit the Vocatio Sebastina in the Cathedral Sebastinum: expend the metered Grace, accept the twenty-fourfold office, and summon the cohort I have named — beginning with the maker from the dying copper world — into Bastion under the lawful conditions of arrival, if the world will grant it.
:::
